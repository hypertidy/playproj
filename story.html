How it works

A story often comes down to one picture: how big a thing would be, standing there. This page sets a block down on real ground. The default is ten storeys of 3 m on a 30 m square, a 30 m cube, in McRobies Gully in Hobart, the valley under kunanyi's foothills that holds the city's tip.

The ground is a square of terrain, a regular mesh in metres on a Lambert azimuthal equal-area plane about its centre, so the block, the ground and the distances share one scale. Its heights come from the Tasmania 2 m DEM, a cloud-optimised GeoTIFF in MGA zone 55 read in place: only the window under the square, from the overview that gives about two cells a mesh cell. GEBCO 2026 fills the sea and anything off the DEM's edge. Drape lays Esri World Imagery over it, read from Web Mercator tiles at the zoom that gives about 2048 pixels across the square, or the LIST's Tasmania orthophoto mosaic (the state's own aerial imagery, a tile cache to zoom 19, about 0.2 m a pixel at Hobart) at about 4096 across. Where the LIST tiles do not load (outside Tasmania, or a browser the server will not share them with) the page says so and uses Esri.

GEBCO 2026 alone shows why the finer DEM matters. GEBCO's cells are 15 arc seconds, about 460 m north to south at Hobart, wider than the whole gully: the valley and its spurs vanish into a gentle tilt, and the block stands on ground that is not there. The readout always gives GEBCO's height under the block, for the comparison. Away from Tasmania, Copernicus GLO-30 (30 m, global) is the next step down from GEBCO; it is not read here yet.

The block stands on the lowest ground under its footprint, so no corner floats and the uphill side is cut into the slope, as a building's would be; the readout says how far the ground climbs across it. Storey lines and windows are drawn at true storey height. Heights are metres above mean sea level, the same for the block and the ground, and the exaggeration, when set, stretches both.

Not for decisions. playproj is a sketchbook for looking at data, not a design or assessment tool. The ground is a public DEM and imagery of their own dates, quantities use typical densities, a pit is spread evenly over a square, and the story figures are quoted from public reporting as given. Use it to get a feel for a size, and go to the primary sources for anything that matters.

How much answers the other story question: what does this many tonnes look like? A tonnage becomes a volume through a bulk density (typical values, listed with each material; soil in place is denser than the same soil loose in a truck), and the volume becomes a cube, a slab of a given footprint, or a pit dug into it, drawn through the ground so it can be seen. Story holds examples, each with where its number comes from: soil from the Macquarie Point stadium site, as the cube it makes at the McRobies Gully tip and as the hole it leaves; a year at the tip's limit; a tonne of CO2.
