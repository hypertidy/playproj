How it works

A map projection takes the ground to a plane by a rule. A picture of the ground does it by a ray: every pixel is a line from the sensor's perspective centre out through the lens, and the pixel shows whatever that line meets first. On flat ground that is a projective transformation (a plane to a plane, which is why four control points can georeference an aerial photo of a flat place). On terrain it is not, because where a ray meets the ground depends on how high the ground is.

That dependence is relief displacement. Follow a ray from the sensor through a point P at height h and carry it on down to a reference surface at height z: it arrives a distance (h - z) tan(theta) beyond P, where theta is the ray's angle from the vertical. So a picture rectified onto a flat datum puts every hill top too far from the nadir (the point straight below the sensor), the higher the further. For a vertical photo from height H that is the textbook D h / (H - h), D the ground distance from the nadir: tall things lean out from the centre of the photo. Straight roads over the hills come out bent.

Orthorectification undoes it with two things: a sensor model (where the sensor was, how it was pointed, how its pixels map to directions) and a DEM. For each cell of the output map, take (x, y), read z from the DEM, and run (x, y, z) forward through the sensor model to an image position, then read the image there. For a frame camera that model is the collinearity equations: with the perspective centre C, the camera's right, down and forward axes r, d, f and the principal distance c (in pixels), u = cx + c (P - C).r / (P - C).f and v = cy + c (P - C).d / (P - C).f. Nothing is traced backwards; the DEM supplies the height the ray could not.

A pushbroom scanner (most Earth observation satellites) has one line of detectors and builds the image a row at a time as it moves along its track. Each row has its own perspective centre, so the model is a line camera per row. With a fixed attitude the scan plane is always square to the track, so relief moves points across the track only: in the raw image the hills lean sideways, never up or down the strip. Real satellite products often ship the model as rational polynomial coefficients (RPCs) fitted to this physical model; GDAL's gdalwarp -rpc -to RPC_DEM=dem.tif is the same loop over output cells as here.

The DEM has to be right. A smoothed DEM leaves the peaks displaced by their height above the smoothing; a level plane at the mean height fixes the average and leaves the rest. And ground the sensor never saw (the back of a ridge, the ground behind a spire) has no pixel of its own: an ordinary orthorectification fills it from whatever was in front, a doubled image of the slope. A true ortho tests each cell against the DEM, marching from the ground towards the sensor, and masks it (magenta) instead. Buildings in city orthophotos are the same story at a smaller scale.

Everything here is in a local frame in metres, x east, y north, z up, on a synthetic terrain over a 10 km square (a DEM on a 50 m grid, read bilinear). The raw image is traced: one ray per pixel, marched to the ground and bisected, coloured by the map (height tint, hill shading, a kilometre grid and four straight roads). The two rectified panels use the same image and the same sensor model, onto the datum (z = 0) and onto the chosen DEM. The code is src/core/ortho.js, checked in test/check-ortho.mjs (the sensor models invert their own rays, displacement is (h - z) tan(theta), and the true DEM reproduces the map).
