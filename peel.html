Is orange peel a sinusoidal map?

Nearly, and the way it falls short is the point. Peel an orange in segments from pole to pole, n of them, and lay each flat. If each parallel keeps its length on the table, a segment's half width at latitude φ is (π / n) cos φ: that is the sinusoidal projection (+proj=sinu) on the segment's own central meridian. Lay the segments side by side, touching at the equator, and they are the interrupted sinusoidal, the map of gores on Globals and interrupteds.

But peel can hardly stretch, and what it would like to keep is length in every direction at once. No flat piece of a sphere can do that, however small (Gauss's Theorema Egregium, 1827: the sphere's curvature is a property of its lengths alone, and the plane's is zero), so every gore is a compromise. The table compares three, each true along its central meridian. The sinusoidal keeps area exactly but shears: towards the poles its edges lean in, and with 12 gores angles there are out by up to 15°. Cassini (cass, true across the gore) and transverse Mercator (tmerc, true in angle) never stretch more than 3.5% for 12 gores. Halve the width and the sinusoidal's shear halves, while the others' stretch falls to a quarter (test/check-peel.mjs checks both rates). So a real segment of peel, which can stretch only a little, sits nearer a Cassini or transverse Mercator gore than a sinusoidal one; all of them meet as the gores narrow, and the sinusoidal is the one that keeps area and lines up into a whole map.

Globe makers have printed gores to paste onto a ball since Waldseemüller's set of 1507, thought to be the first printed (Library of Congress). Their gores often stop short of the poles, which get round caps instead, where a narrow gore is all shear.

The spiral peel

Peel it the other way, in one strip of even width w wound from pole to pole, latitude rising by w each turn. The strip is about 4π / w radii long, the sphere's area over its width. Laid flat, a narrow strip keeps the curvature its centre line had on the sphere (its geodesic curvature), and at latitude φ that is tan φ. Measured by arc length t from the equator, sin φ = t / a with a = 2π / w, so the flat line turns at t / √(a² − t²). Near the equator that is t / a, curvature growing in step with arc length: an Euler spiral (a clothoid), whose coordinates are the Fresnel integrals, scaled by √(2a). This is Bartholdi and Henriques (2012), Orange peels and Fresnel integrals. The blue line is the Euler spiral; the readout says how much of the strip, from the middle out, stays within half a strip's width of it. The ends, near the poles, curl tighter than the Euler spiral does, and the narrower the strip, the smaller the share it matches but the closer the match there once scaled.

A spiral peel is not a map projection in the usual sense: it is one long piece, every point of the sphere is on it once, and nothing on it is straight. The gores are the opposite trade: many pieces, each nearly a map.

How it works

Gores: each one is a grid in longitude and latitude, flattened by its projection (sinu, cass or tmerc on the sphere, following PROJ and checked against PROJ 9.8 in test/check-peel.mjs). The peel opens the flower: each gore is wrapped on a sphere of radius 1 / (1 − peel) that touches the orange at its south pole, with each point's distance from the pole and its direction there blended from the orange to the flat gore. At 0 it is the orange, at 1 the flat flower.

Spiral: the flat centre line is integrated from its heading a − √(a² − t²), and the strip is the centre line with its width either side (held inside the radius of curvature near the poles, where the flat strip would fold over itself). The peel comes off from the south pole; the peeled part lies flat in the plane that touches the orange where it is coming away, matched to the strip's direction there.

The distortion figures are worked from the gore's own formulas by finite differences: area is the worst error in areal scale, angles the worst angular distortion, stretch the largest error in scale in any direction, and edge how much longer the gore's flat edge is than the meridian it came from (π radii).
