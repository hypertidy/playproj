How it works

On Mercator the coast of East Antarctica runs right across the bottom of the map, wider than Australia two or three times over. Mercator keeps angles by stretching every parallel to the length of the equator, so at latitude φ everything is drawn sec φ times too big: 1.1 times across the middle of Australia, 2.5 times on the Antarctic Circle, 3 times at 70°S, and without end at the pole. There are two ways to ask how long the coast really is.

Across. A coast that spans Δλ degrees of longitude at latitude φ is about R cos φ Δλ across, where Mercator draws R Δλ, the same as at the equator. The territory's sectors take 109° of longitude, two and a half times what Australia's mainland takes, but they sit near 67°S, where a degree of longitude is less than half as long. Across, the two are much the same size. A parallel draws any one parallel with its true and drawn lengths.

Around. A coastline has no one length. Walk a pair of dividers along it, opened to a ruler r, and count the steps: the shorter the ruler, the more of the bays, capes and glacier tongues it follows, and the longer the coast. Lewis Fry Richardson measured coasts and borders this way and found the length grows as a power of the ruler, L ~ r1−D; Benoit Mandelbrot read D as a fractal dimension (1 for a smooth curve, more for a wigglier one; about 1.25 for the west coast of Britain, 1.02 for South Africa's). The plot is log length against log ruler, with D fitted from the slope between 10 and 500 km. Below a few km these coastlines, generalised for maps at 1:10 million, have no more detail to follow and the curve flattens: Geoscience Australia's 35 821 km for the mainland comes from maps at 1:100 000.

Which coast. Much of Antarctica's edge is floating ice. The grounding line is where land and grounded ice meet the sea or the shelf floating on it; the ice front is the edge a ship meets, the seaward edge of the ice shelves. Under the Ross and Ronne-Filchner shelves the grounding line is near 84°S, where Mercator's stretch is past 10, so the two coasts differ more on Mercator than on the earth.

The Australian Antarctic Territory is the sectors 45°E to 136°E and 142°E to 160°E, south of 60°S; Adelie Land, between, is claimed by France. Australia here is the mainland, without Tasmania and the islands. Lengths are on the sphere of mean radius (6371.0088 km), the divider steps as great-circle arcs; on the WGS84 ellipsoid they differ by well under one percent (test/check-coastlen.mjs). "As Mercator draws it" sums each step as the straight line between its Mercator ends, read at the equator's scale.
