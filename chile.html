    How it works

    Chile is about 4 300 km from Arica to Cape Horn and seldom more than 350 km across, but the mainland runs from 66.4 W to 75.7 W, across the meridian 72 W where UTM zone 19S meets 18S. The official grid is UTM on SIRGAS-Chile: SIRGAS-Chile 2021 / UTM zone 19S (EPSG:20049), 18S (EPSG:20048) and 12S for Easter Island (EPSG:20042). The Ministerio de Bienes Nacionales survey manual (2025) sets UTM for the whole country except the Chilean Antarctic Territory, which is left to the projection the national cartographic authority decides. SIRGAS-Chile has been realised again after the great earthquakes (2002, 2010, 2013, 2016, 2021 in EPSG, with a 2025.0 epoch now in use), so the same point has a slightly different coordinate in each one. Every grid on this page is on GRS80, which is what those realisations use.

    The shading is PROJ's proj_factors() for the chosen grid at each pixel. Area is the areal scale s: a cell that is c by c metres on the map covers c2/s on the ground, so the readout gives the error in square metres for the chosen cell size. Distance is the linear scale in the direction where it is worst (a or b, in parts per million). Shape is the angular distortion, the most any angle is bent. The table at the top sums them over the land: 7 700 points 0.1 degrees apart inside mainland Chile.

    No grid wins all three. A conformal grid (UTM, any transverse Mercator, the Lambert conic) has no shape error, and its area error is the square of its scale error. An equal-area grid (Albers, Lambert azimuthal) has no area error at all, whatever its parameters, and its parameters only move the shape error around. For a country this long and narrow, a cone (Albers, Lambert conformal conic) is the wrong surface: it is true along parallels and Chile runs along meridians. A transverse Mercator on the middle of the country keeps scale within 0.2 percent from end to end, and the Lambert azimuthal centred on it bends shapes by at most 1.6 degrees.

    Other long countries made the same choice in different ways. Argentina divides its land into seven Gauss-Kruger strips with k = 1 (POSGAR 2007, Faja 1 on 72 W is EPSG:5343). New Zealand has one national transverse Mercator, NZTM2000, and keeps local meridional circuits for survey. Norway maps in UTM and builds in NTM, 1 degree transverse Mercator zones. Brazil has an official equal-area grid for statistics, SIRGAS 2000 / Brazil Albers (EPSG:10857). In Chile a 2015 proposal from the roads directorate suggested 1 degree local transverse Mercator zones for engineering, but it has not been adopted.

    src/core/chile.js has the grids and the land summary; the projections are Snyder (1987) and Karney (2011), checked against PROJ in test/check-chile.mjs. The page grew out of an area comparison for Chile in this gist.
